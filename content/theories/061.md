## 61. Pull-Up Bar: Installation and Safety

### EN: Short
A pull-up bar lets you do pull-ups, chin-ups and hanging exercises, but only if it is securely installed and rated for how you use it. How well it is mounted matters more than how many reps you can do.

### EN: Detailed
**Mounting types.** Doorframe leverage bars, expandable tension bars, fixed wall-mounted bars and ceiling-mounted bars each install differently. A bar sold as “doorway compatible” will not fit every door trim, wall or frame. Some types need structural fasteners into solid supports, and others depend on a specific frame shape.

**Check the rating and the structure.** Read the manufacturer’s instructions and weight limit, including whether dynamic movements are allowed. Count your full body weight plus the extra force of swinging or pulling. Inspect the screws, brackets, padding and the surfaces the bar touches or sits near. If you are unsure about the building structure or the mounting method, ask a qualified installer.

**Test it step by step.** Before a full hang, make sure the bar is seated correctly and cannot rotate, slide or shift. Keep your feet on the floor and press down on it gently and with control. Move on to a partial hang, then a full supported hang, only once you know the installation is sound. A short test cannot prove that damaged hardware or an unsuitable frame is safe.

**Common hazards.** Do not attach bands or do kipping, swinging or weighted pull-ups unless the product is designed and installed for exactly that. Leave room for your head, elbows and feet, with no glass or sharp furniture nearby. Stop using a bar that creaks, slips, bends or loosens.

**Using TrainUp.** In Profile, mark your pull-up bar as safely installed only after you have checked all of the above. Until then, your plan leaves out exercises that need the bar, and rows or band exercises with a secure anchor train the same pull. If you are a beginner, supported hangs, scapular pulls or assisted pull-ups are good first steps once the bar is safe.

## 107. Strength Training for Teenagers

### EN: Short
Supervised strength training designed for your age can help you build coordination, strength and confidence in how you move. Good technique and gradual progress come first, and copying an adult’s max-lifting program is the wrong model.

### EN: Detailed
**What the evidence shows.** The American Academy of Pediatrics considers well-designed youth resistance training beneficial, with low injury rates when instruction and supervision are good. Properly supervised resistance training has not been shown to stunt height growth or damage growth plates just because weights are used.

**Start with movement basics.** Bodyweight squats, push-up variations, rows, jumping and landing practice, and light resistance exercises teach control. Practice symmetrical and single-leg movements, learn how to handle the equipment, and make things harder based on your technique and maturity, not on what your friends are lifting.

**The full activity picture.** CDC guidance for ages 6–17 recommends at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity every day, including muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on at least three days a week. Strength sessions are one part of that, alongside sport, play and recreation.

**Why supervision matters.** Qualified coaching, equipment that fits your age, enough rest and a safe space all cut avoidable risks. Avoid max attempts without supervision, and don’t rush your progression or take on advanced lifting by yourself. Involve a parent, guardian or qualified coach where you can. If you have pain, a significant medical condition or worries about your development, ask a doctor. No training plan can guarantee growth, injury prevention or performance results.

**Sources:** [American Academy of Pediatrics: youth resistance training](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/6/e20201010/76942/); [CDC: physical activity for children and teens](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/children.html).

## 84. Static and Dynamic Stretching

### EN: Short
Static stretching holds a position, and dynamic stretching moves through a range under control. Both can help you prepare to move or improve flexibility when you match them to your goal and keep the intensity comfortable.

### EN: Detailed
**Static stretching.** A static stretch gently holds a muscle or joint near the end of its comfortable range. A calf stretch against a wall or a supported hip-flexor stretch, for example, can be held for a short time without bouncing. It should feel like mild tension, never sharp pain or numbness.

**Dynamic stretching.** Dynamic stretching moves through a useful range again and again: leg swings you can control, arm circles, alternating lunges. Each rep is deliberate, not bouncing or rushed. These drills fit naturally into a warm-up for running, lifting and sports.

**When to use each.** Before explosive effort, gradual dynamic movement and practice of the exercise itself usually work best. Long, intense static stretching right before may briefly reduce force or power, while a few short holds are less of a concern. After training or in a separate session, static stretching is a reasonable way to work on flexibility.

**How much to do.** Use short, comfortable holds and repeat them over weeks instead of forcing a new range in one session. For dynamic drills, start with a small range and widen it only while the movement stays controlled. Stretching does not need to hurt to work, and more flexibility does not help every task.

**What stretching can’t promise.** Use dynamic drills to get ready for active movement and static holds when flexibility is the goal. Neither one makes you injury-proof or reliably prevents soreness. If pain persists, ask a doctor or physiotherapist for advice that fits you.

## 20. Rep Ranges and What They Train

### EN: Short
Rep ranges guide what you emphasize, but they don’t split training into strict categories. Heavier, low-rep work matters most for maximal strength, muscle can grow across many ranges, and high reps can build local muscular endurance.

### EN: Detailed
**A starting framework.** Low rep ranges, often around one to five per set, usually go with heavier loads and plenty of rest for maximal strength practice. Moderate ranges, roughly six to twelve, make it easy to combine a solid load, technique control and total work. Higher ranges, such as fifteen or more, can help with local muscular endurance and lower-load training.

**Why the ranges overlap.** Research does not support the idea that six to twelve reps are the only way to build muscle, or that fifteen reps can’t build strength. Heavy loading works especially well for improving maximal one-rep strength, while muscle can grow across a wide range of loads when effort and volume are high enough. Very high reps may be cut short by discomfort or by heart and lung fatigue before the target muscle is challenged enough.

**Matching the range to the exercise.** A heavy compound lift may be easier to control for fewer reps. A bodyweight squat may need more reps before it gets hard. A plank is usually measured by how long you hold it. The intended movement quality, safety, the weights you have and your equipment should guide the choice.

**Example.** A beginner might do 2–3 sets of 8–12 goblet squats, rows and presses, keeping a few reps in reserve. If the same resistance gets easy for twelve reps, raise the load slightly or pick a harder variation. Someone who later focuses on strength may do more sets of fewer reps in chosen lifts.

**Common mistake.** A rep target means little if you ignore how close the set comes to a hard effort. Ten effortless reps and ten reps near your limit are different doses of training.

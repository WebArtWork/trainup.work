## 73. Interval Training

### EN: Short
Interval training alternates harder work with easier recovery. By setting how long and how hard each part is, you can improve your conditioning step by step, and no session has to be an all-out test.

### EN: Detailed
**How intervals work.** A work interval raises your breathing and workload, and the recovery interval lets you recover partly or mostly. You can adjust both. Intervals can be aerobic, such as brisk walking alternating with easy walking, or harder, such as a fast cycling bout followed by easy pedaling.

**Start below maximal effort.** If you are new to exercise, you could alternate one minute of brisk walking with two minutes of comfortable walking for six rounds. An experienced runner might use faster efforts with longer rest. The right work speed depends on your level and the exercise, so a time ratio alone does not tell you how hard the work is for your body.

**Recovery length depends on the goal.** Longer recoveries keep your speed and movement quality high on intense reps. Shorter recoveries make the session more continuous and may raise the overall demand on your heart and lungs, but if they are too short, quality drops fast. Set work and rest to match the conditioning goal you have in mind.

**Control the total load.** Always warm up gradually and finish with something easier. Build up the total amount of interval work before making the intervals much harder. If your walking form, running mechanics or jump-rope coordination starts to break down, take longer recovery, lower the intensity or end the session.

**Using TrainUp.** The rest timer and hold timer keep your intervals on time, and after the workout you rate the session’s effort from 1 to 10. Harder is not automatically better. When you regenerate your plan, it gets easier if 2 of your last 3 workouts were very hard (RPE 9 or more) or less than 60% finished, and a step harder only if the last 4 were easy and complete. If you need less impact, cycling or fast marching work as intervals too.

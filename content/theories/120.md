## 120. Myth: No Pain, No Gain

### EN: Short
Effort and gradually harder training build fitness, but you don’t need pain or severe soreness to improve. Aim for a challenge you can control, good technique and recovery, and don’t measure success by how much you suffer.

### EN: Detailed
**Effort and harm.** Muscle fatigue, heavy breathing and a temporary burn can come with hard exercise. Sharp pain, new joint discomfort, pain that spreads or dizziness are not achievements. Your body adapts when a hard but tolerable workout is followed by enough recovery. Pain is not what makes it work.

**Soreness is a poor score.** Delayed-onset muscle soreness is especially common after unfamiliar or hard exercise. It often fades as your body gets used to a movement, while your strength and fitness keep improving. A session that leaves you barely sore can still be productive, and too much soreness can make it hard to train consistently.

**Progress without punishment.** Pick reps that are challenging but controlled, raise training demands gradually, and include rest or easier days. Finishing a set with a few reps in reserve may be sensible. Research does not show that the average healthy adult needs to reach momentary muscular failure again and again to get worthwhile results.

**Example.** A beginner who does two full-body workouts a week and steadily adds squat and push-up reps can progress without feeling wrecked after every session.

**Using TrainUp.** After a workout, you rate the session’s effort (RPE) and report any pain with the body area and exercise. If something hurts during a workout, use “I feel pain” to end the exercise at once; it stays out of your plans until you resume it from Profile. Any recent pain means your next plan won’t get harder. New or severe pain is a reason to stop and get it assessed.

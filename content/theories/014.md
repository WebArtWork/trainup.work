## 14. Energy Systems in Exercise

### EN: Short
Exercise draws on three overlapping energy systems: phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative. All three work all the time, but how much each one matters changes with how hard and how long you move and how much you recover between efforts.

### EN: Detailed
**Immediate high-power energy.** The ATP-phosphocreatine (phosphagen) system can refill ATP quickly during short, high-power efforts such as a jump, a sprint start or a heavy set. It can keep maximum output going for only a short time, which helps explain why several minutes of rest can restore performance between hard strength or speed efforts.

**Fast carbohydrate breakdown.** Anaerobic glycolysis supplies ATP from carbohydrate, and the reactions that produce it don’t need oxygen. It matters most during hard efforts that last from many seconds to a few minutes. Lactate is often called “waste” or blamed as the only cause of soreness, but your body also uses it as fuel and as a metabolic signal.

**Sustained aerobic production.** Oxidative metabolism in the mitochondria uses oxygen to make ATP from carbohydrates and fats. It powers long walks, cycling, steady running and much of the recovery between hard intervals. Even short efforts get some aerobic contribution, because the systems never switch on and off in separate time boxes.

**Why duration and rest matter.** A near-maximal five-second sprint and a 30-minute walk need very different amounts of power. Repeated 20-second hard intervals with short rests make it harder to keep up your output as fatigue builds. Heavy barbell sets usually need longer rests so you keep your force and technique, and getting as breathless as possible is not the aim.

**Putting it into practice.** Give yourself enough recovery when force or speed is the main target. Use steady moderate activity to build aerobic capacity. Use intervals for conditioning when they fit, and set the work and rest to your current ability. A high heart rate during lifting does not mean the session gave you the same aerobic stimulus as sustained cardio.

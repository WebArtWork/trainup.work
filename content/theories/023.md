## 23. Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE)

### EN: Short
Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) is your own rating of how hard exercise feels. If you record it next to what you completed, you can spot sessions that were easier or harder than expected and adjust future training a little at a time.

### EN: Detailed
**Two common uses.** Session RPE rates the overall difficulty of a whole workout, often on a 0–10 category-ratio style scale that runs from rest to maximal effort. Set RPE rates one resistance-training set. The two measure different things: one set can be extremely hard inside a session that felt moderate overall.

**Set RPE and reps in reserve.** One strength-training system ties each rating to the number of reps you think you had left. RPE 10 means you could not have done another full rep with the intended technique, RPE 9 means one was left, and RPE 8 means about two were left. These are estimates, not precise physiological measurements, and you get better at them with training experience.

**Examples.** A brisk 25-minute walk may feel like 4/10 for one person and 7/10 for a deconditioned beginner. A set of eight squats with about two more reps possible would be roughly RPE 8 on the reps-in-reserve lifting scale. Always say which scale you are using, or the numbers get confusing.

**Common errors.** Ratings lose their value if you rate effort only by how much you sweat, mix up muscle soreness with how hard the session was, or always pick 10 because a workout “should be hard.” Sleep, stress, heat and unfamiliar movements all change how hard exercise feels, so read each rating in its context.

**Turning ratings into decisions.** If several sessions feel much harder than planned, look at your sleep, load, number of sets, rest and recovery. If sessions keep feeling very easy and your technique is solid, try a small increase next time. One unusual rating is not enough to diagnose overtraining or to make big changes.

**Using TrainUp.** After each workout you rate the whole session from 1 to 10. Treat that number as session effort, and don’t convert it into set RPE or reps in reserve. Look at your ratings next to how much of each workout you finished, over several workouts.

**Sources:** [Zourdos et al. RIR-based RPE research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/); [Helms et al. practical RPE guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27531969/); [CDC exertion guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/measuring/).

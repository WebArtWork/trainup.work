## 52. Supersets and Circuits

### EN: Short
A superset pairs exercises with little rest between them, and a circuit rotates through several movements. Both can save time, but the fatigue and shorter recovery may lower the quality of heavy or very technical exercises.

### EN: Detailed
**Definitions.** An antagonist superset alternates opposing movement patterns, such as a press followed by a row. A compound set pairs two exercises for the same muscle group. A circuit might rotate through five stations before you rest and repeat. EMOM means “every minute on the minute,” and AMRAP means “as many rounds or repetitions as possible” in a set time.

**Why use them.** Pairing a push with a pull lets one muscle group work while another rests, which can save time. Circuits can combine resistance and cardio work in a small space. Many people assume they build more strength or muscle, but they don’t automatically; the quality of the total work and your recovery still decide that.

**Manage fatigue.** A heavy squat paired with a hard deadlift can make both lifts worse, especially for beginners. Pair exercises that don’t compete too much, and rest long enough to stay in control. Technical lifts and explosive jumps often need more recovery than a timed circuit gives. Use supersets when you are short on time and the pairing makes sense for your goal. Don’t cut rest just to hit a time target, and skip circuits full of jumps or tricky moves if your space, noise limits or body rule them out.

**Example.** Do ten controlled incline push-ups, then ten supported rows, then rest 90 seconds; repeat three times. A conditioning circuit could use step-ups, a light carry and marching, with planned rest. Choose a number of rounds you can keep up without rushing into poor technique.

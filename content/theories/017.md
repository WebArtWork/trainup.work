## 17. Motor Learning: How Technique Is Built

### EN: Short
Exercise technique is a skill you build through repeated, attentive practice and useful feedback. You learn exercises more efficiently when you start with easier versions, keep your reps consistent, and make things harder only once you have control.

### EN: Detailed
**How skills develop.** Early on, you have to think about basic positions and order: where to put your feet, how to move your hips, how to guide your elbows. With practice, movements become more consistent and need less attention. At an advanced level, you can adapt the learned movement to different loads and situations, which takes more than memorizing one perfect-looking rep.

**Practice quality.** Frequent accurate reps with a load you can control give you more useful learning than occasional chaotic all-out attempts. Start with a version of the exercise you can control. Progress through range, resistance, balance demands or speed only after the current version is repeatable.

**External and internal cues.** An internal cue might be “tighten your glutes,” and an external cue might be “push the floor away.” Different cues work for different people and movements. Use one short cue at a time, because a dozen body-part instructions in one rep are too many to manage.

**Feedback.** Video from a helpful angle can show uneven depth, balance problems or unwanted torso movement, though camera angles can mislead. A mirror can help you sense your position, but you don’t need to watch it constantly. A coach is worth it for unfamiliar complex lifts or technique problems that keep coming back.

**Fatigue changes learning.** High fatigue can reduce precision, so do new or high-skill exercises near the start of the workout. Later in the session, familiar and simpler exercises fit better. End a set when your technique becomes uncontrolled, and don’t treat every breakdown as productive effort.

**Using TrainUp.** Each exercise page in Explore has photos, steps and technique notes, and the plan picks exercises that fit your level and coordination. Record the reps you actually did, and use the workout notes to write down whether they were controlled.

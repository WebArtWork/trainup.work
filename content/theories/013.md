## 13. The Spine, Core and Bracing

### EN: Short
Your spine supports you and lets you move, and the trunk muscles around it help control forces during exercise. Bracing is a coordinated tightening of the trunk that can improve stability, and it is different from holding one rigid posture in every activity.

### EN: Detailed
**Basic anatomy.** The spine has cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccygeal regions. Its natural curves differ between people and change with position. In many loaded lifts, keeping your spine controlled and close to your own comfortable neutral range is a useful starting strategy, especially while you are learning.

**What the “core” includes.** The abdominals, obliques, deeper trunk muscles, back extensors, diaphragm and pelvic floor muscles work together to manage pressure and movement. They bend the trunk, and they also resist unwanted extension, rotation or side bending while force passes between your arms and legs.

**Bracing and hollowing.** Bracing usually means creating tension all the way around your trunk, as if getting ready for a gentle push to the stomach, while keeping your breathing under control. Hollowing focuses on drawing the abdominal wall inward. Both can have uses, but they are different instructions. For heavy lifting, coordinated bracing usually helps more than just pulling the stomach in.

**Intra-abdominal pressure.** Your diaphragm and abdominal wall can create internal pressure that helps stiffen the trunk. The exact breathing strategy depends on the load and your experience. Long, intense breath-holding may not suit people with certain cardiovascular conditions, so if that applies to you, ask your doctor for advice.

**Example.** For a moderate goblet squat, stand tall, take a comfortable breath, tighten your torso, lower under control, and breathe out on the hard way up if you need to. For a suitcase carry, keep your trunk upright and don’t let the weight pull you sideways.

**Training the core.** Core work covers planks, dead bugs, carries and the main movement patterns as well as sit-ups. No single spinal position is safe for everyone, and bracing does not make up for choosing a sensible load.

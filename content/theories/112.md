## 112. Measuring Strength Progress

### EN: Short
Strength progress can show up as more reps, heavier loads or harder variations of an exercise. A fair comparison keeps your technique and conditions the same, and a personal record is only one of the numbers that count.

### EN: Detailed
**Ways to measure.** A new load record is easy to spot, but 10 controlled reps with a weight that used to allow seven is progress too. With bodyweight exercises, you can improve by lowering the incline of a push-up, adding range of motion or moving from assisted to unassisted reps.

**Estimated and tested maximums.** Your one-rep max (1RM) is the heaviest load you can lift for one rep with acceptable technique. Formulas can estimate it from lighter sets, but the estimates get less reliable across exercises, at higher rep counts and between lifters. Beginners don’t need max tests to track progress.

**Compare like with like.** Keep grip, depth, tempo, equipment and rest about the same when you compare sessions. A heavier partial squat isn’t automatically equal to a lighter full-range squat. More control, confidence or endurance at the same load also counts as improvement, with or without a new max.

**Example.** A row goes from 3 × 8 to 3 × 12 with the same dumbbell and similar technique. You could then try a small load increase and work back up through the rep range.

**Using TrainUp.** TrainUp doesn’t prescribe weights: you choose the load. Your plan gives sets and reps for each exercise, and during the workout you record the reps you actually did.

## 33. The Hip Hinge Pattern

### EN: Short
A hip hinge bends mainly at the hips while you keep your trunk controlled. It is the base of deadlifts, Romanian deadlifts and kettlebell movements, and it trains the glutes and hamstrings to produce force.

### EN: Detailed
**Learn the motion.** Stand with soft knees. Push your hips back as if closing a car door with them, and let your torso tip forward while your back stays in a comfortable, controlled position. Stop when your hamstrings limit the movement or your trunk can’t stay controlled, then drive your hips forward to stand. You are training hip motion, so reaching the floor is not the target.

**Hinge and squat.** Both movements use the hips and knees, with a different emphasis. In a squat the knees usually bend more and the torso stays more upright. In a Romanian deadlift the knees bend a little and the hips move farther back, which puts more work on the back of the body. A glute bridge trains hip extension with your torso supported and is sometimes an easier place to start.

**Teaching drills.** For a wall tap, stand a short step away from a wall and push your hips back until they touch it. A light dowel held along your head, upper back and pelvis can tell you when your spine moves a lot. Use these drills for learning, and don’t force a rigid posture because of them.

**Progression and mistakes.** Start with unloaded reps, then move to a light kettlebell deadlift or a dumbbell Romanian deadlift. Keep the weight close and use a range you can control. Bending the knees too much turns the lift into a squat, and chasing a set depth may move the motion into your back. Swings need more skill than a slow hinge.

**Using TrainUp.** Your plan includes hinge exercises that fit your equipment and goal. If balance or a sensitive back makes loaded hinges hard, a supported variation or a hip bridge is an option, and you can note a back limitation in Profile. If pain persists, see a doctor or physiotherapist instead of building your own rehab plan.

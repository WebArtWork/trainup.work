## 63. Choosing the Right Load

### EN: Short
The right load is heavy enough to make you work and light enough that every rep stays controlled. As a beginner, you can choose weights by how well you move and how many reps you have left, without testing a maximal lift.

### EN: Detailed
**Start with an easy rehearsal.** Do the movement with no weight or with the lightest implement you have. Add a small load for a warm-up set and check that your range of motion, breathing and tempo stay steady. Each movement has its own working weight: a load that feels comfortable for a row may be far too heavy for a lateral raise.

**Reps in reserve.** Reps in reserve (RIR) is how many more good-form reps you think you could still do. For general training, ending many sets with about two or three reps left is a practical starting point, though you do not have to hit it every time. If a planned set of 10 feels like you could do 10 more, the load may be too light for your goal.

**Adjust during the session.** Say the plan calls for three sets of eight to twelve. If your first set ends at eight with shaky technique and nothing left, lower the load. If twelve reps are smooth and still fairly easy, try a small increase on the next set. Rest long enough to tell whether the weight or the fatigue caused the problem.

**When the weight jumps are too big.** If the next dumbbell is too heavy, add a rep, a controlled pause or another good set. If even your lightest weight is too heavy, use bodyweight, a resistance band or a different leverage. Never grind out failed reps to reach a set number.

**Using TrainUp.** Your plan gives sets, reps and rest but no weights, so you choose the load. During the workout, record the reps you actually did, and after it, rate the session’s effort from 1 to 10. If you feel pain, get dizzy or keep losing your technique, stop or change the exercise; do not add resistance. “I feel pain” ends the current exercise at once.

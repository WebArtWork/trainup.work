## 111. Keeping a Training Log

### EN: Short
A training log records what you planned, what you did and how it felt. Keeping it regularly helps you tell real progress from normal day-to-day ups and downs, and gives you better information when you change your program.

### EN: Detailed
**What to record.** For strength work, note the exercise, variation, load, reps and completed sets, plus rest when it matters. For timed exercises, note the duration and how well you held the position. For conditioning, note the activity, duration, and distance or pace. A quick session RPE and a short comment can explain an unusual result.

**Make entries comparable.** “Push-up 10 reps” is hard to read if the hand height or range changes every session. Log the variation and conditions so you compare like with like. Keep it simple: a few fields you fill in every time are better than a long form you give up on.

**Add notes for context.** Poor sleep, unfamiliar equipment, interruptions or new discomfort can explain a weaker session. One bad day is no reason to overhaul the whole program. Trends over several sessions show whether the plan is getting too easy, too hard or is about right.

**Example.** Week one: goblet squat, 3 × 8 at 12 kg, moderate effort. Week three: 3 × 10 at the same load with good technique. That’s a measurable improvement even if your body weight hasn’t changed.

**Using TrainUp.** During a workout you check off each set and record your reps. Afterward you rate the session’s effort, report any pain and add notes. History shows this week, your total workouts, average effort and a 4-week chart, and finished workouts are never changed. Your plan changes only when you regenerate it.

**Sources:** [2026 ACSM position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/); [ACSM 2026 practical guidance](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf).

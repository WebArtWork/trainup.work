## 21. Training Volume

### EN: Short
Training volume is how much work you do. In resistance training it is usually counted as hard sets per muscle group per week. Enough volume drives progress, but sets beyond what you can recover from may lower the quality of your training.

### EN: Detailed
**Ways to count work.** The simplest count is the number of working sets. “Volume load” multiplies sets × repetitions × weight, so three sets of ten at 40 kg give 1,200 kg of volume load for that exercise. This helps when you compare the same movement over time. It does not work across exercises: a machine press and a bodyweight push-up don’t translate into the same kilograms of stimulus.

**Hard sets per muscle.** For muscle growth, coaches often count the hard sets that load each muscle group. Compound lifts count toward several muscles, though not always equally. A squat strongly trains the knee and hip extensors, while a row adds work for the back and arms. If you count every muscle involved as if it got the same stimulus, your totals will come out too high.

**How many sets to do.** The 2026 ACSM position stand found that higher weekly volumes, including at least about ten sets per muscle group, can increase hypertrophy. That is not a starting dose for everyone, and it doesn’t guarantee progress. Beginners often improve with fewer sets. More experienced trainees may benefit from more, if time, technique and recovery allow.

**Build gradually.** A new trainee might start with two sets each of several patterns per session, twice a week. After some weeks of steady performance, add a set to a lagging pattern only if soreness, sleep and session quality allow it. Jumping from six to twenty hard weekly sets for one muscle adds fatigue risk for no good reason.

**Quality and quantity.** Sets done with poor technique, little effort or heavy fatigue still raise a spreadsheet total, but they add little. Steady, consistent progress matters more than having the highest volume.

**Using TrainUp.** In each workout you check off your sets and record your reps, and History shows your average effort and a 4-week chart. Compare similar weeks. If effort is higher than expected or your reps are dropping, look at your volume before you add more.

**Sources:** [2026 ACSM position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/); [Hypertrophy mechanisms review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35389932/).
